# Craft

Structure and optimization decide whether a piece gets found. Craft decides whether it gets finished, shared, linked, and believed. Both matter; only one of them is a moat.

---

## Headlines

**The core rule, arrived at independently by nearly every operator who tested it: clear beats clever.** The reader should know exactly what they're getting before they click. A headline that requires a click to understand is a headline that doesn't get clicked.

**For search:** primary keyword early, ~50–60 characters, promise stated plainly.
**For social and platforms:** more room for specificity, curiosity, and stakes.
**These can differ.** The `<title>` tag, the H1, and the social share title are three different jobs and can carry three different strings.

### Patterns that hold up

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Straight informational | What is / How to + topic | How to build a topic cluster |
| Numbered specific | Number + specific noun + qualifier | 7 internal linking mistakes that cost us 40% of our traffic |
| Result-led | Outcome + timeframe + method | How we tripled organic traffic in 9 months without buying links |
| Contrarian | Common belief + correction | Word count doesn't matter. Here's what does. |
| Comparison | X vs Y for Z | Semrush vs Ahrefs for solo bloggers |
| Definitive | The complete/definitive guide to X | The complete guide to generative engine optimization |
| Question | The exact question searched | How long does SEO take to work? |

### Rules

- Write 10 headlines, pick one. The first is always the most generic.
- Numbers outperform, consistently. Use odd, specific ones.
- Specificity beats superlatives. "40% of our traffic" beats "amazing results."
- Never promise what the body doesn't deliver. One bought click costs several future ones.
- Test the fifth-grader standard: could a twelve-year-old say what this article is about?

## Article structures

### The definitive guide (2,500–5,000 words)

Best for pillar pages and head terms.

1. **Answer-first block** (40–60 words) — direct answer to the core question
2. **Why this matters** (150–300) — stakes, with a real number
3. **The main body** — 6–12 H2 sections, each a question, each self-contained
4. **Tables, diagrams, examples** throughout
5. **Common mistakes** — a high-value, high-share section almost everyone skips
6. **What to do next** — specific, actionable
7. **FAQ** — only real questions, only if genuinely useful; this is prime AI-extraction real estate

### The comparison (1,200–2,000)

Best for commercial intent. Highest conversion value of any format.

1. Verdict up front — who should pick what, in three sentences
2. Comparison table
3. Each option: what it's best at, what it's bad at, who it's for
4. The decision framework — how to choose, not just what exists
5. Honest limitations of your own recommendation

Publishing the case against your favorite is what makes the rest believable.

### The how-to (800–1,800)

1. What you'll accomplish and what you need
2. Numbered steps, one action each
3. Screenshots or code for every non-obvious step
4. What goes wrong and how to fix it
5. What to do next

### The original research / data piece (1,500–3,000)

The best link and citation magnet available.

1. The headline finding, up front
2. Methodology — sample, method, dates, limitations
3. Findings, one per section, each with a chart
4. What it means and what you'd do about it
5. The raw data, downloadable

### The teardown / case study (1,500–2,500)

1. The result, with real numbers
2. The starting position
3. What was done, chronologically
4. What worked, what didn't, what was luck
5. What's replicable for the reader

Honesty about what was luck or an unfair advantage is what makes this format trusted.

## The universal spine

Regardless of format:

**Answer first.** A direct, self-contained 40–60 word answer to the core question, before anything else. This satisfies impatient readers, wins featured snippets, and is the chunk AI engines extract. Burying it under 800 words of preamble is the most common self-inflicted wound in blogging.

**Then earn the depth.** Once the answer is delivered, the reader who stays is the reader who wants more — write for them, not for the bouncer.

**Self-contained sections.** Each H2 section should make sense lifted out of context, because that's what happens to it. No "as mentioned above."

**Evidence in every section.** A number, a source, an example, or a firsthand account. An unsupported paragraph is a paragraph that gets skipped and never cited.

**Show the work.** Screenshots, real data, actual outputs. This is E-E-A-T made visible.

**End with an action**, not a summary. The reader already read it.

## Opening lines

**Delete on sight:**
- "In today's fast-paced world..." and every variant
- "In this article, we will explore..."
- Defining a term before establishing why anyone should care
- Any sentence whose only content is that you're about to say something
- Apologizing for the gap since your last post

**Replace with one of these:**
- **The answer.** Just say it. Then expand.
- **A number.** "Sixty-one percent of the subscribers who joined last year have never opened an email."
- **A flat contradiction.** "Word count is not a ranking factor. It never was."
- **The reader's situation.** "You've published forty posts and nothing ranks. Here's what's usually wrong."
- **A specific scene.** "In March we deleted 200 pages from a client site. Traffic went up 30%."

## Voice

Blog writing fails in the same way as most professional writing: it sounds like nobody.

- **First person, positions taken.** "I think X is wrong, here's what I'd do instead" is worth ten paragraphs of balanced overview.
- **Specificity is the whole craft.** "A big company" → "a 400-person Series C fintech." "Recently" → "in March." "A lot" → "40%."
- **Short paragraphs, 2–4 sentences.** Extractability and mobile readability are the same problem.
- **Vary sentence length.** Land key claims short.
- **Admit uncertainty and failure.** "We tried this and it didn't work" is the most credible sentence in content marketing.
- **Cut connective tissue.** "In order to," "it's important to note that," "at the end of the day," "let's dive in."

## The generic-writing pass

These patterns make writing sound machine-produced whether or not a machine was involved. They're also, not coincidentally, exactly what AI engines deprioritize as low-information-gain.

**Delete or rewrite:**
- "delve," "tapestry," "landscape of," "navigate the," "unlock the," "seamlessly," "robust," "game-changer," "ever-evolving," "at the intersection of," "in the realm of"
- "It's important to note that," "furthermore," "moreover," "in conclusion"
- **The rule-of-three tic** — every list having exactly three items
- **"It's not just X, it's Y"** and its symmetrical cousins. Once per piece maximum, usually zero.
- **Summary paragraphs restating what you just said**
- **Balanced non-conclusions** — "there are compelling arguments on both sides"
- **Uniform paragraph length** — real writing has ragged rhythm

**Add back:**
- A specific detail only you would know
- An opinion you'd defend at dinner
- A real number from your own work
- Something that didn't work
- The word "I"

## Formatting for extraction and skimming

The same choices serve both:

- H2 every 250–400 words, phrased as a question
- Paragraphs of 2–4 sentences
- Bold the sentence you'd want caught while skimming — sparingly, or it stops working
- Comparison tables for anything with more than two options
- Numbered lists for sequences, bullets for genuinely parallel items
- Custom diagrams, not stock photos — diagrams earn links and screenshots; stock photos earn nothing
- Pull quotes and callout boxes to reset attention in long pieces
- Descriptive anchor text on every link, never "click here"

## Editing, in order

**Pass 1 — structure.** What is the one idea? Where does it actually start (usually paragraph 3–5)? Where does it sag? Is the best material buried in the middle?

**Pass 2 — evidence.** Does every claim have a number, source, or example? Anything you'd have to fudge to defend? Cut or verify it.

**Pass 3 — line.** Cut hedges, filler openers, adverbs doing a verb's job. Convert abstractions to specifics. Read aloud; every stumble is a reader's stumble.

**Pass 4 — generic-writing pass.** The list above.

**Pass 5 — mechanical.** Run `scripts/content_audit.py`. Title, meta, slug, alt text, internal links, schema.

Do them separately. Line editing a section you should delete is wasted work.
