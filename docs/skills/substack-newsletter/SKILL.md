# Newsletter & Substack

A system for producing newsletters that people actually open, finish, and forward — built from the observable patterns of writers who have done it at scale.

The core belief behind everything here: **a newsletter is a promise that gets kept on a schedule.** Growth tactics amplify a kept promise and accelerate the death of a broken one. So positioning and craft come first, distribution second.

---

## Step 0 — Establish the promise before writing a word

Never draft into a vacuum. If the user hasn't given you these, either infer them from context (their existing posts, their bio, the conversation) or ask for the missing ones in a single batch. Do not ask more than about four questions — get moving.

1. **Who is the reader?** Not a demographic — a person in a situation. "Series-A founders deciding whether to hire a VP Sales" beats "startup people."
2. **What is the recurring promise?** The thing every issue delivers. If you can't say it in one sentence, the publication has no spine.
3. **Why this writer?** The unfair advantage — access, experience, taste, obsession, or a genuinely weird angle.
4. **What is the cadence and the format?** Weekly deep dive is a different animal from daily digest.
5. **What does success look like?** Subscribers, clients, book deal, paid conversion, or just thinking out loud. This changes the CTA and the whole architecture.

If the user is starting from zero, walk them through `assets/voice-brief.md` — it produces a positioning statement, three content pillars, and a one-line promise you can reuse in every subsequent draft.

**The positioning test:** a good newsletter promise is one a reader could describe to a friend in one sentence, and that sentence would make the friend either subscribe or clearly not. If both people would shrug, the positioning is too broad.

## Step 1 — Pick the format

Match the format to the promise and the writer's actual capacity. Full anatomies, section-by-section, live in `references/formats.md` — read it before drafting anything longer than a few hundred words.

| Format | Best for | Anchor examples | Typical length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analytical deep dive** | Explaining why something happened, one idea per issue | Stratechery, The Diff | 1,500–3,000 |
| **Narrative deep dive** | Company/category stories with a thesis | Not Boring, The Generalist | 3,000–8,000 |
| **Wry running commentary** | Daily/frequent takes on a beat | Money Stuff | 2,000–4,000 |
| **Practical operator post** | Answering a job-shaped question | Lenny's Newsletter, Mostly Metrics | 1,200–2,500 |
| **Personal essay** | Culture, identity, meaning-making | Culture Study, most of literary Substack | 1,200–2,500 |
| **Contextual dispatch** | Recurring events explained through a lens | Letters from an American | 600–1,200 |
| **Curated digest** | Saving the reader time across sources | Morning Brew, The Browser | 500–1,200 |
| **Teardown / reverse-engineering** | Showing your work on someone else's success | Growth In Reverse | 2,000–4,000 |
| **Modular short** | Consistency with low overhead | 3-2-1 style, link-and-thought roundups | 300–800 |

Rules of thumb:
- **Pick one primary format and repeat it.** Recognizability is the whole game — readers subscribe to a shape, not just a topic.
- Length follows the idea, not the format label. Never pad to hit a word count. A tight 900 words beats a bloated 2,400 every time.
- A publication can carry one secondary format (e.g. a Friday links roundup alongside a Tuesday deep dive), but not three.

## Step 2 — Draft

Read `references/frameworks.md` for the specific writer systems — how Thompson builds an argument, how Levine sustains a voice, how McCormick structures a narrative arc, how Gabriele front-loads a brief. Use them as scaffolding for the user's own voice, never as an impression to imitate.

The universal spine, regardless of format:

**Hook (first 1–3 sentences).** The reader is deciding right now whether to keep going. Open on the specific — a scene, a number, a claim, a contradiction, a question they've privately had. Never open with throat-clearing ("In today's fast-moving world...", "I've been thinking a lot about..."), never with an apology for the gap since the last issue, never with a table of contents.

**Turn (paragraph 2–4).** Name the tension. Why is the obvious answer wrong, incomplete, or more interesting than it looks? This is the promise that carries the reader into the body.

**Body.** One idea, developed. Nearly every weak newsletter draft is three half-ideas wearing a trenchcoat — cut two of them and go deeper on the survivor. Move in short sections with concrete evidence: numbers, quotes, screenshots, examples, personal experience. Every abstraction needs a specific within two sentences of it.

**Turn out.** Zoom from the specific to what it means. This is where a newsletter earns forwards — the reader wants to hand a *conclusion* to someone, not a summary.

**Close.** A line the reader remembers, then one clear ask. One. Reply, share, subscribe, or click — pick the single one that matters for this issue.

`references/hooks.md` has hook patterns, subject line construction, and preview-text strategy. Read it whenever you're writing openings or titles, which is most of the time.

### Voice discipline

The differentiator between newsletters that grow and newsletters that stall is almost never information — it's the presence of a person. Concretely:

- **Write in first person and take positions.** "I think X is wrong, and here's what I'd do instead" is worth ten paragraphs of balanced overview. Readers subscribe to judgment.
- **Short sentences carry the weight.** Vary rhythm, but land your key claims short.
- **Use "you" liberally.** It's a letter, not a report.
- **Show the seams.** What surprised you, what you got wrong, what you're still unsure about. Uncertainty stated plainly reads as credibility.
- **Specificity is the entire craft.** "A big company" → "a 400-person Series C fintech." "Recently" → "in March." "Grew a lot" → "from 11 to 5,800 in six months."
- **Cut the connective tissue.** Delete "in order to," "it's important to note that," "at the end of the day," "let's dive in." They add words and remove voice.

### Structural formatting for email

Email is read on a phone, half-attentively, in a queue of other emails. So:

- Paragraphs of 1–3 sentences. White space is a reading aid, not a design choice.
- Bold sparingly, for the sentence you'd want someone to catch while skimming. If everything is bold, nothing is.
- Subheads every 200–400 words, written as *statements* ("Distribution is the moat") not labels ("Distribution").
- Lists only when items are genuinely parallel. Prose carries argument better than bullets do, and a post that's all bullets reads as notes rather than thinking.
- Images, charts, or pull quotes every few screens in long pieces — they reset attention.

## Step 3 — Edit

Drafting and editing are different jobs; do them separately. Run `references/editing-checklist.md` on any draft — yours or the user's. It covers the structural pass, the line pass, the AI-tell removal pass, and the pre-send checklist.

The two highest-leverage edits, always:

1. **Cut the first two paragraphs.** They are usually the writer warming up. The real opening is often paragraph three.
2. **Read it aloud.** Anywhere you stumble, a reader stumbles. Anywhere you sound like a press release, rewrite in the words you'd use to explain it to a friend over coffee.

## Step 4 — Package

An issue isn't finished when the body is done. Every send needs:

- **Subject line** — 3–7 words, ~30–50 characters so it survives mobile truncation. Specific and curiosity-driven, never both vague and clickbait. Numbers and concrete nouns outperform adjectives. Write 8–10 candidates and pick; don't write one and ship it.
- **Preview text** — the second headline, not a repeat of the first. Extends the subject line rather than restating it.
- **Title** (if it differs from the subject — on Substack it usually should, since the web title also does SEO work)
- **Subtitle / dek** — one sentence that makes the promise explicit
- **One CTA**
- **3–5 Notes or social posts** derived from the issue — atomized ideas, not "new post is up" links

When the user asks for a full issue, deliver all of these, not just the body.

## Step 5 — Growth and money (only when asked)

Don't bolt growth advice onto a drafting request. When the user *does* ask — "how do I grow this", "should I go paid", "how do I get to 1,000" — read `references/growth.md` and `references/monetization.md`.

The 30-second version, so you're never useless without them:

- **Free list first, paid second.** Paid conversion is a function of a trusting free list, not the other way around. Roughly 5–10% of an engaged free list converts; below ~1,000 engaged subscribers, a paywall usually costs more in growth than it earns.
- **On Substack, the network is the growth engine**, not luck. Notes, Recommendations, cross-posts, and guest swaps drive most growth under 10k subscribers. Collaboration compounds; solo posting alone does not.
- **One entry point.** A signature series or flagship piece that new readers get pointed to, plus a lead magnet that delivers a fast, concrete win.
- **Consistency beats volume.** A predictable weekly send outperforms a sporadic firehose, because the promise is what's being kept.

## Working with the user's own writing

When editing or reviewing rather than drafting:

- **Preserve their voice.** Your job is to make them sound more like themselves, not more like a newsletter. Flag habits, don't overwrite personality. If they write long, tangential, comma-heavy sentences and it *works*, leave it.
- **Lead with what's working.** Name the strongest paragraph specifically before naming problems — it tells them what to do more of, which is more actionable than a list of fixes.
- **Diagnose, don't just prescribe.** "The hook buries the interesting claim in paragraph four" is more useful than "punchier hook."
- **Give one structural note, then line notes.** Structure first — no point polishing sentences in a section that should be cut.

## When metrics come up

Open rates are unreliable post-Apple Mail Privacy Protection — inflated and non-comparable across platforms. Treat click-to-open rate, reply rate, and paid conversion as the honest signals, and treat any specific benchmark number the user cites as directional at best. If they're chasing an open-rate number, redirect them to whether people are *replying*.
