# Growth

Two things have to be true at once: you consistently publish something worth sharing, and you deliberately place it in front of people who've never heard of you. Most stalled newsletters are doing exactly one of these.

Note on currency: platform mechanics change fast. The principles below are durable; if the user is acting on a specific feature or number, verify it's still accurate rather than assuming.

---

## Stage-appropriate priorities

| Stage | Focus | Ignore |
|---|---|---|
| 0–100 | Positioning, first 10 issues, personal outreach | Everything else |
| 100–1,000 | Notes/social consistency, recommendations, first collaborations | Monetization, paid ads |
| 1,000–10,000 | Guest swaps, lead magnets, signature series, SEO | — |
| 10,000+ | Paid tier, sponsorships, products, referrals | — |

The most common mistake is running 10,000-subscriber tactics at 200 subscribers.

---

## The Substack network is the growth engine

For publications under roughly 10,000 subscribers, the platform's own network — Notes, Recommendations, cross-posts, and writer collaborations — drives the majority of growth. These are not optional extras; treating them as such is why most Substacks stall in the low hundreds.

### Notes

The fastest free growth channel on the platform, and the one most writers use wrong.

**Wrong:** posting "new issue is live 👇" with a link. Link posts are deprioritized nearly everywhere, and they ask for a click before giving anything.

**Right:** treat Notes as a standalone short-form channel. Post ideas, observations, excerpts, questions, and disagreements that are complete in themselves. Distribution follows engagement, and engagement follows things people want to reply to.

- 3–5 Notes per week, minimum. Daily if you can.
- Reply to every comment on your own Notes.
- Spend more time in *other people's* Notes than your own. Substantive replies to writers in your niche is the highest-leverage activity available to a small publication.
- Atomize each issue into 3–5 Notes over the following week. One post should never generate one promo.
- Restacks with commentary — adding your own take rather than bare resharing — perform far better and build relationships with the original author.

### Recommendations

Writers recommending each other is the platform's compounding loop. The mechanics:

- Recommend publications you actually read. Readers notice mismatches.
- Reciprocity is common but not automatic. Recommend first, without asking, then reach out.
- Relevance beats size. A recommendation from a tightly-aligned 3,000-subscriber publication converts better than a loose one from 50,000.
- Recommendations compound: as your list grows, your recommendation becomes more valuable, which makes larger writers willing to trade.

### Collaborations

Consistently the highest-yield growth activity available. Practitioners commonly report guest exchanges producing subscriber counts that dwarf a typical week of organic growth — often 60–80+ per exchange, versus single-digit or low-double-digit organic days.

**Formats, roughly in order of effort:**
- **Restack + commentary** — lowest effort, builds the relationship
- **Cross-promotion** — share another writer's post to your list
- **Quote/feature them** in a piece and tell them
- **Comment swap** — genuinely engage in each other's comment sections for weeks
- **Interview** them for your publication
- **Guest post swap** — each writes an issue for the other's list
- **Co-written piece** — highest effort, highest yield
- **Joint live session or pop-up newsletter**

**How to approach.** Read their last five posts. Reference something specific. Lead with what you'll give, not what you want. Propose one concrete format with a date. Do not send a template.

Make collaboration a standing monthly commitment, not a one-off experiment. The writers who compound treat it as a permanent part of the calendar.

### Live video

Sends a notification to your entire list without algorithmic filtering, and generates clips as a byproduct. Underused relative to its leverage. Even 15 minutes monthly counts.

## Off-platform

**LinkedIn / X / Threads / TikTok.** Repurpose, don't cross-post. Rewrite each idea in native form for the platform. One issue should yield 5–10 pieces of social content.

**SEO.** Slow but compounding. Optimize the web title, the URL slug, the meta description (~160 characters), and use H2 subheads that match how people actually search. "How to" and "what is" titles earn passive subscribers for years. Expect 3–6 months before it registers.

**Podcasts and YouTube.** Guest appearances convert unusually well because the audience has heard you think for an hour before they subscribe.

**Communities.** Slack groups, Discords, subreddits, forums where your reader already is. Be useful for weeks before you mention the newsletter, and even then mention it once.

## Entry points

A newsletter needs a door — one obvious thing a new reader is pointed to.

**Signature series.** A recurring flagship format with a consistent title pattern that new readers can binge. This is what turns "I liked that post" into "I need to be subscribed."

**Lead magnet.** The current pattern that works: a fast, concrete win, not a 40-page ebook. Length is negatively correlated with conversion. A swipe file, a checklist, a template, a small dataset, a five-day pop-up newsletter, or literally a piece of the thing you sell.

- Repurposing existing high-performing content into a magnet often outperforms building something new.
- "Comment to get" mechanics on social — reply to a post and the magnet is DM'd — work well right now.
- Promote the magnet continuously. Most people build one and mention it twice. It belongs in your bio, your About page, the footer of every issue, and periodically in Notes.

**About page.** Most-visited page after your latest post, and usually the worst-written thing on the site. It needs: who this is for (specifically), what they get, how often, who you are and why you, and a subscribe button above the fold. Not your résumé.

**Homepage.** Above the fold, a visitor should learn in five seconds what the promise is and why you. Feature the signature series. Kill anything else.

## Onboarding

Passive subscribers from recommendation networks are lower-intent than direct signups and can drag deliverability if you ignore them. Onboarding is a growth strategy, not an afterthought.

A five-email welcome sequence — see `assets/welcome-sequence.md` — should:
1. Deliver the magnet immediately and set expectations
2. Send your best-ever post
3. Introduce you as a person
4. Ask a question and invite a reply (replies are the strongest deliverability signal available)
5. Present the paid tier or the community

**List hygiene.** Periodically remove subscribers who haven't opened in 6+ months. It feels bad and it improves everything — deliverability, honest metrics, and your own sanity.

## Metrics that mean something

Open rates are unreliable. Apple Mail Privacy Protection pre-loads tracking pixels, inflating reported opens; the same real engagement can show as 21% on one platform and 49% on another. Benchmarks quoted without stating their MPP treatment aren't comparable.

**Use instead:**
- **Click-to-open rate** — did the people who opened care?
- **Reply rate** — the strongest signal of a real relationship, and the best deliverability input
- **Share/forward rate** — is this worth passing on?
- **Free → paid conversion** — the honest business metric
- **Subscriber source attribution** — which channel actually works
- **Growth rate net of unsubscribes**

A/B testing on subject lines is still valid despite MPP inflation, since both variants are inflated equally.

---

## Consistency

The most replicated finding across every successful newsletter: reliability beats volume. A weekly send that never misses outperforms a sporadic one at three times the volume, because what you're actually building is a habit on the reader's side. Pick a cadence you can hold at your worst month, not your best.
