# Monetization

The list is the asset. The newsletter is how you earn it. Money comes from the relationship, not only from the words.

---

## Sequencing

Paid conversion is a function of free-list trust, not of content quality in isolation. Build the free list first.

**Rough readiness signals for a paid tier:**
- 1,000+ engaged subscribers (engaged, not raw)
- Consistent publishing for 6+ months without gaps
- Regular replies and comments from readers you don't know
- People asking questions your free content can't answer
- A clear sense of what the paid thing *is*

Below that, a paywall usually costs more in growth and momentum than it earns.

**Expected conversion:** roughly 5–10% of an engaged free list. Under 1,000 subscribers, do the arithmetic before you build the tier.

---

## Pricing

Two viable strategies, and they're genuinely opposed:

**Low price × broad audience.** ~$5/month for a large consumer audience with daily or near-daily value. The highest-revenue publication on Substack runs this model — millions of subscribers at the lowest entry price on the platform. Works when the audience is huge and the habit is daily.

**Premium price × professional audience.** $15–30/month when the reader's employer benefits. Common in B2B: a $15/month product tier plus an expense-it tier at a multiple of the price, plus an annual bundle of partner tool subscriptions worth several times the subscription. B2B readers have a budget, not a wallet — price to the budget.

**Standard conventions:** annual billing at a 10–20% discount is near-universal. Founding-member tiers at 3–5× the normal price convert a meaningful number of true fans and are almost free to offer. Launch promotions and referral discounts are set by the writer, not the platform.

**Pricing errors:** underpricing because you feel awkward (the most common), and pricing above what your archive justifies. If someone can't see the value from your free archive, the price isn't the problem.

## Paywall models

| Model | What's paid | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency split** | Some issues free, some paid | Deep dives, analysis |
| **Depth split** | Free preview, paid full analysis | Research-heavy work |
| **Community** | Threads, comments, chat, live sessions | Personal essay, identity-based audiences |
| **Archive** | Recent free, back catalogue paid | Publications with deep evergreen archives |
| **Utility** | Templates, data, benchmarks, tools | B2B and operator audiences |
| **Access** | AMAs, office hours, direct contact | Expert-led publications |

**Choose based on what your readers actually want more of.** If your comment section is alive, sell community. If people ask for your spreadsheets, sell utility. If they want more of you specifically, sell access. Don't guess — read your replies.

**Free content still has to be excellent.** The free tier is the marketing. A paywall over mediocre free content converts nobody.

## The launch sequence

Do not turn on paid quietly and hope. A launch is a multi-week sequence.

**4 weeks out.** Start referencing that a paid tier is coming. Ask readers what they'd want in it — this is both research and pre-commitment.

**2 weeks out.** Publish your single best free piece. Not a lesser one held back — your actual best. It's the proof.

**1 week out.** Announce the date, the price, and precisely what's included. Offer a founding-member tier.

**Launch day.** A dedicated send. Explain honestly why you're doing it and what it enables. Give a founding discount with a real deadline.

**Days 2–7.** One reminder. Share early founding members' names if they consent. Then stop.

**Ongoing.** A permanent, tasteful paid CTA in every issue. Occasional paywalled posts with a genuinely valuable free preview. Never a guilt trip.

**Tone.** Confidence without apology. "This takes 20 hours a week and I'd like to keep doing it" is honest and works. Apologizing for asking teaches readers that asking was wrong.

## Non-subscription revenue

Often larger than subscriptions, and available earlier.

**Sponsorships.** Viable from around 5,000 engaged subscribers. Rates vary widely by niche — B2B and finance audiences command far more than general interest. Sell the audience, not the impressions. Formats: primary placement, classified section, dedicated advertorial send (highest rate, use sparingly). Take fewer, better sponsors — reader trust is the inventory you're actually selling, and it doesn't replenish.

**Products.** Courses, cohorts, templates, tools, books. The newsletter validates demand before you build: whatever readers ask about most is the product.

**Services.** Consulting, advisory, ghostwriting, speaking. For many B2B writers this is the largest line by a wide margin, and the newsletter is functionally lead generation.

**Community.** Paid Discord, Slack, or cohort. High retention, high maintenance — don't start one you won't show up in.

**Adjacent businesses.** Funds, agencies, job boards, events, podcasts. Several top newsletters are primarily distribution for a business the writer already runs. This is legitimate and often the highest-margin path.

**Affiliates.** Fine in small doses if genuinely used and disclosed. Corrosive at scale.

## Retention

Churn is the silent killer. Everything about acquisition is irrelevant if the back door is open.

- **Cadence.** Missed sends are the leading cause of churn. Reliability is the product.
- **Onboarding.** New paid subscribers should get an immediate, specific welcome — what's coming, how to get the most out of it, how to reach you.
- **Ongoing proof.** Regularly remind paid subscribers what they're getting. People forget what they pay for.
- **Annual > monthly.** Longer commitment, less churn decision-making, better cash flow. Discount accordingly.
- **Exit surveys.** One question when someone cancels. The answers are more useful than any growth tactic.
- **Pause, don't cancel.** Offer a pause option. Many take it and come back.

---

## The honest framing

Most newsletters do not become full-time businesses, and that's not a failure. Newsletters that consistently make real money have one of: an unusually large audience, an audience with budget, an adjacent business the list feeds, or several years of compounding. Be clear with the user about which of these they're actually building toward — vague monetization hope produces vague, unfocused publications.
