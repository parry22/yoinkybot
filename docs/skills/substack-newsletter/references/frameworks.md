# Writer Frameworks

Reverse-engineered patterns from operators who have built newsletters at scale. Each entry: what they do, why it works, the transferable mechanic, and when it's the wrong tool.

These are **scaffolds, not costumes.** Borrow the structure and the discipline. Do not attempt pastiche of anyone's voice — imitation reads as thin, and the whole value of a newsletter is that a specific person is behind it.

## 1. Ben Thompson — Stratechery

**The publication:** Daily tech strategy analysis. Roughly 40,000+ paid subscribers as a one-person operation, ~$3M/year. Fifteen-year archive. The most-cited independent business analyst on the internet.

**The system: proprietary vocabulary as a moat.**

Thompson's real product isn't the news analysis — it's a set of named frameworks (Aggregation Theory, conservation of attractive profits, the fight for the user) that readers adopt and then use in their own thinking. Once your reader argues *in your vocabulary*, you're not a newsletter anymore, you're infrastructure. This is the single most durable moat in the newsletter business.

**Structural pattern — the four-move essay:**

1. **News peg.** Something happened. Stated flatly, briefly, with a link. Two or three sentences, no drama.
2. **The obvious reading.** What everyone thinks this means. Stated fairly and generously — steelmanned, not strawmanned.
3. **The reframe.** Why the obvious reading misses the structural point, connected to a prior framework. This is the payload.
4. **The implication.** What follows if the reframe is right — for the company, the category, the reader's own strategy.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Never cover press releases; cover inflection points.** The question isn't "what happened" but "what does this reveal about how the system works." If you can't answer the second, don't write it.
- **Name your recurring ideas.** When you find yourself explaining the same concept for the third time, give it a name and link back to the original piece forever after. The name compounds; the explanation doesn't.
- **Link to your own archive relentlessly.** Every named concept should be one click from its origin essay. This turns depth into a retention mechanism and makes new readers spelunk.
- **First-principles openings.** Start from how the business model actually works, not from the discourse.
- **Consistency as product.** Daily, on schedule, for years. The archive *is* the moat; each individual post is a deposit.

**Use when:** the beat has recurring structural dynamics — tech, markets, policy, industries. Anywhere the same forces produce different surface events.

**Don't use when:** your value is emotional, aesthetic, or narrative. Framework-first writing on personal topics reads cold.

## 2. Matt Levine — Money Stuff

**The publication:** Near-daily finance column with the format and behavior of a newsletter. Widely considered the best financial writing on the internet and the most-cited voice on market structure.

**The system: voice as the product.**

Levine covers subject matter — securities law, derivatives, corporate governance — that is objectively dry, and makes it the most enjoyable read in the category. The mechanism is that the *voice* is the reason to open, not the topics. Readers who don't care about a given deal read the item anyway.

**Voice mechanics worth stealing:**

- **The recurring bit.** Running jokes and stock phrases that recur across issues ("everything is securities fraud" energy). Readers who get the reference feel like insiders — this is the cheapest, strongest in-group builder available to a writer.
- **Deadpan absurdity.** Describe genuinely strange behavior in flat, precise language and let the strangeness do the work. Never announce that something is funny.
- **Consistent first-person and genuine curiosity.** He's audibly delighted by weird financial engineering. Enthusiasm about your own beat is contagious and nearly impossible to fake.
- **Long parenthetical asides and footnotes** that function as a second, more intimate channel of voice.
- **The multi-item structure.** Several unrelated items per issue, each with its own subhead, each self-contained. Weak items don't sink the issue; readers skim to what interests them.

**Structural pattern — the item:**

Set the scene in one or two sentences → present the odd fact → explain the mechanism clearly enough for a non-expert → step back to the general principle it illustrates → land on a dry punchline.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Build recurring bits deliberately.** After three issues, notice what you keep saying and lean into it. In-jokes are retention.
- **Explain the mechanism, always.** Never assume the reader knows the machinery. The explanation *is* the value even for expert readers, who enjoy watching it done well.
- **Multi-item format is a hedge** — good for high-frequency publications where not every day yields a big idea.

**Use when:** you publish frequently, your beat generates a steady stream of small strange items, and you have or want a distinctive voice.

**Don't use when:** you publish weekly or less. Multi-item at low frequency reads as a lack of a thesis.

## 3. Packy McCormick — Not Boring

**The publication:** ~265,000 subscribers. Monday deep dives of 5,000–10,000 words on a company, category, or theme. Advertising plus a venture fund built off the audience.

**The system: business strategy with the pacing of sports writing.**

McCormick's own framing was that Not Boring is what you get if Ben Thompson and Bill Simmons had a child — rigorous strategy analysis delivered with the energy, humor, pop-culture texture, and relentless optimism of great sports columns. The insight is that a crowded category (tech strategy) can be re-entered on *tone* rather than information.

**Structural pattern — the narrative deep dive:**

1. **Cold open.** A scene, an anecdote, or a genuinely surprising claim. Often personal, often funny. Never a definition.
2. **The promise.** Explicitly tell the reader what they'll understand by the end and why it matters. He is unembarrassed about saying "this is going to be a long one, here's why it's worth it."
3. **Table of contents** for very long pieces — permission to skim, which paradoxically increases completion.
4. **Build from primitives.** Start with how the thing actually works at the simplest level, then layer complexity. The reader is never lost, which is why non-experts finish 8,000 words on a category they've never heard of.
5. **Analogies and pop-culture hooks** to make abstract mechanics concrete.
6. **The thesis, stated loud.** He commits. Optimism as an explicit stance, with the reasoning exposed so you can disagree with it.
7. **Zoom out.** What this means for the future, for builders, for the reader.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Enter a crowded category on tone.** If the incumbent is rigorous and dry, be rigorous and fun. Differentiation by personality is more defensible than differentiation by information, because information gets commoditized.
- **Optimism is a position.** Cynicism is the default internet register and therefore not distinctive. Enthusiasm, held sincerely, is rare.
- **Build from primitives** whenever writing for a mixed-expertise audience. Assume intelligence, don't assume context.
- **Length is fine if the pacing is right.** Long pieces fail from monotony, not word count. Vary section length, insert images, break rhythm.
- **Never fake the persona.** McCormick's optimism works because it's real and visible everywhere else he shows up. A performed personality collapses under weekly publishing.

**Use when:** deep dives on companies, categories, or technologies for an audience that isn't uniformly expert.

**Don't use when:** your readers are time-poor specialists who want the answer in 400 words.

## 4. Mario Gabriele — The Generalist

**The publication:** Exhaustively researched long-form deep dives on companies and investors. Each issue reads like a small magazine — market maps, frameworks, original interviews.

**The system: front-loaded briefs and structural navigation.**

Where McCormick pulls you through with pace, Gabriele respects that you may not have 40 minutes. Pieces open with a tight summary of the takeaways — a "brief" or bulleted thesis block — so a reader gets 80% of the value in two minutes and chooses whether to spend the other 38.

**Structural pattern:**

1. **The brief.** 3–6 bullets, each a substantive conclusion, not a topic label. "Company X's real moat is its underwriting data, not its brand" — not "we discuss the company's moat."
2. **Origin story.** Founders, founding conditions, the counterfactual.
3. **The mechanics.** Product, business model, unit economics, with numbers.
4. **The competitive landscape.** Often a visual map.
5. **The thesis and the risks.** Both sides, explicitly.
6. **What to watch.** Falsifiable predictions and signposts.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Write the summary as conclusions, not contents.** This is the highest-value edit available on any long piece. Most writers write a table of contents and call it a summary.
- **Consistent section architecture across issues.** Readers learn where things live and can navigate. Recognizability compounds across a body of work.
- **Original research is the moat.** Interviews, primary documents, data you assembled. Anyone can synthesize public commentary; almost no one does the calls.
- **Include the bear case.** Publishing the strongest argument against your own thesis raises credibility more than any amount of hedging.
- **Design counts at this length.** Charts, maps, clean typography. A 6,000-word wall of text won't be read regardless of quality.

**Use when:** research-heavy pieces where the reader might be a professional making a decision.

**Don't use when:** you can't do original research. This format punishes thin sourcing.

## 5. Lenny Rachitsky — Lenny's Newsletter

**The publication:** Over 1M subscribers, the largest product-management publication in the world. Paid at $15/month with an "I can expense it" tier and an annual bundle of partner tool subscriptions worth multiples of the price.

**The system: answer job-shaped questions, then productize the answers.**

Every post answers a question a specific professional has actually asked, in a form they can act on Monday. The topics come from the audience — reader questions, polls, and direct requests — which means demand is validated before the work is done.

**Structural pattern — the operator post:**

1. **The question, verbatim.** Often literally a reader's question. Instant relevance.
2. **Why it's hard / why the common answer fails.**
3. **The answer, structured.** Numbered steps, a framework, or a decision tree.
4. **Evidence from practitioners.** Quotes and examples from named people who've done it — this is the differentiator versus generic advice.
5. **Templates, benchmarks, or artifacts** the reader can copy.
6. **Caveats and when this doesn't apply.**

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Source topics from your audience.** Ask directly, run polls, mine your replies and DMs. Never guess at demand.
- **Aggregate expertise rather than only your own.** Interviewing 15 practitioners and synthesizing is a repeatable engine that scales past your personal experience.
- **Ship artifacts.** Templates, spreadsheets, checklists, benchmark data. Artifacts get saved and shared in Slack, which is distribution that outlives the send.
- **Price to the buyer's context.** The expense-it tier works because B2B readers have a budget, not a wallet. Bundling partner tools makes the price arithmetically obvious.
- **Titles are search queries.** "How to X" and "What to do when Y" earn compounding search traffic that essayistic titles never will.

**Use when:** B2B, professional, or skill-based audiences with concrete recurring problems.

**Don't use when:** the value is perspective rather than utility — this structure flattens good essays into listicles.

## 6. Heather Cox Richardson — Letters from an American

**The publication:** The most-subscribed paid Substack, estimated 2.9M+ subscribers and the highest revenue on the platform, at one of the lowest prices ($5/month). Nightly.

**The system: one lens, applied relentlessly, forever.**

A historian explains the day's political events by placing them in historical context. That's it. Same lens, every single night, for years. The constraint is the product: readers know exactly what they're getting and exactly why she's the one to give it.

**Structural pattern:**

1. Today's event, stated plainly.
2. The historical parallel or precedent.
3. What the history reveals that the news coverage doesn't.
4. Where the thread goes next.

Delivered in calm, plain, unhurried prose. No hot takes, no urgency theater, no design flourishes.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **A narrow lens beats broad coverage.** "Current events through the lens of American history" is defensible in a way that "politics commentary" never is. Ask: what do I see that others structurally cannot?
- **Extreme cadence discipline builds ritual.** Nightly makes it a habit, not a media product. Habits don't churn.
- **Low price × massive volume** is a legitimate strategy when the audience is broad and the value is daily. Don't assume premium pricing.
- **Calm is a differentiator** in loud categories. Emotional register is a positioning choice most writers never consciously make.
- **Consistency is the moat.** Nothing here is unreplicable except the fact that she has done it every night for years.

**Use when:** you have genuine domain expertise that reframes something people already care about.

**Don't use when:** you can't sustain the cadence. Broken ritual is worse than no ritual.

## 7. Anne Helen Petersen — Culture Study

**The publication:** Cultural criticism and personal essay; strong paid community with active comment sections and threads.

**The system: rigorous thinking in an intimate register, with the community as a feature.**

Petersen's posts read like a very smart friend working something out, but they're structurally argumentative — the intimacy is a delivery mechanism for analysis, not a substitute for it.

**Structural pattern — the essay:**

1. **A specific, often mundane observation.** A thing she noticed, a phrase she keeps hearing, a personal experience.
2. **The generalization.** This isn't just me — here's the pattern.
3. **The analysis.** Structural, economic, historical causes. This is where it earns its keep.
4. **Complication.** What's messy, what she's uncertain about, who this framing fails.
5. **Open question to the reader** — which drives the comment section, which is the paid product.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Personal as an entry point, not the destination.** The anecdote buys attention; the analysis keeps it. Pure personal writing has a low ceiling unless the writer is unusually interesting.
- **Name a feeling people have but haven't articulated.** This is the single highest-share mechanic in personal/cultural writing. Coining a term for a common unnamed experience creates enormous forward-share.
- **Threads and comments as the paid tier.** Access to the community rather than access to the content — a good paywall for audiences that want to talk to each other.
- **Uncertainty as credibility.** "I'm not sure about this part" reads as honest and invites reply.

**Use when:** culture, identity, work-life, meaning — anywhere the reader's own experience is the subject.

**Don't use when:** the reader needs a decision, not a perspective.

## 8. Chenell Basilio — Growth In Reverse

**The publication:** 40,000+ subscribers. Deep teardowns reverse-engineering how top newsletter creators grew.

**The system: do the research nobody else will, and show all of it.**

Each issue involves many hours of digging — archives, interviews, timelines, traffic sources — to reconstruct exactly how someone grew. The moat is effort, publicly visible.

**Structural pattern — the teardown:**

1. **The result up front.** The number that makes you want the story. "How X added 1,749 subscribers in 30 days."
2. **Who they are and why they're worth studying.**
3. **The timeline.** Growth reconstructed chronologically, with the inflection points marked.
4. **The mechanisms.** Each specific tactic, named and explained.
5. **What's replicable vs. what was luck or unfair advantage.** The intellectual honesty that makes it trustworthy.
6. **Your action items.** What the reader does this week.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Teardowns are the most reliable format for a new newsletter in any category.** You don't need to be an authority — you need to be a rigorous student. Analyze the best in your field publicly and you build authority by proximity.
- **Lead with the number.** Concrete results in the title are the highest-performing hook in any results-oriented category.
- **Separate replicable from unrepeatable.** Advice that admits "this only worked because he already had 200k on Twitter" is trusted far more than advice that doesn't.
- **Effort is a moat.** In a world of fast synthesis, forty hours of original research is genuinely hard to copy.

**Use when:** entering a category where you're not yet the authority.

## 9. Sahil Bloom / James Clear — modular shorts

**The publication:** Short, tightly structured, high-frequency. Clear's 3-2-1 format (three ideas, two quotes, one question) reaches millions.

**The system: a fixed container that removes the blank page.**

The format is identical every week. This does three things at once: makes the send instantly recognizable, makes the writing sustainable for years, and makes each unit independently shareable.

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Invent a container and never change it.** "Three things I noticed this week," "one question, one number, one link" — the specific shape matters less than that it's fixed.
- **Modularity creates atomized distribution.** Each item works as a standalone social post. One issue becomes five Notes.
- **Constraint improves quality.** Forcing an idea into 100 words removes the padding that longer formats tolerate.
- **Sustainability is a strategy.** A format you can produce in 90 minutes for five years beats one that takes ten hours and burns you out in four months.

**Use when:** you want consistency without high overhead, or you're publishing alongside a job.

**Don't use when:** your value is depth and argument — the format caps how deep you can go.

## 10. Morning Brew — the digest voice

**The publication:** Daily business news digest. The template most modern digest newsletters descend from.

**The system: save time, be funny, sound like a person who works there.**

The value proposition is time saved. The retention mechanism is voice — jokes, casual register, a consistent editorial personality that survives writer turnover because it's documented in a style guide.

**Structural pattern:**

- Warm, specific opener referencing the day or something in the news
- Items in order of importance, each with a punchy headline
- Each item: what happened → why it matters → the wry aside
- Recurring sections readers navigate by habit
- Light, skimmable close

**Transferable mechanics:**

- **Curation is a real product** if you have taste and consistency. Selection is the value; commentary is the differentiation.
- **"Why it matters" is mandatory.** A link roundup without interpretation is an RSS feed. Two sentences of "here's what this actually means" is the whole job.
- **Write a voice guide.** Literally document the register, the jokes you do and don't make, the words you don't use. This makes voice reproducible and delegable.
- **Recurring sections train navigation.** Readers who know where their favorite bit lives open more reliably.

**Use when:** a beat with high news volume where readers are drowning.

**Don't use when:** you can't add interpretation — you'll be replaced by an aggregator.

## 11. Byrne Hobart — The Diff

**The system: connect domains nobody connects.**

Finance × technology × history, fused in every piece. The differentiation is *combinatorial* — plenty of people write about markets, plenty about tech, almost nobody with genuine fluency in both plus historical pattern recognition.

**Transferable mechanic:**

- **Positioning by intersection.** If you're a top-10% writer in two or three unrelated domains, the intersection is uncontested — and more defensible than being top-1% in one. This is the single most useful positioning move for a writer who feels like they lack a niche. "I'm interested in too many things" is a strength if you fuse them consistently rather than alternating between them.
- **Fuse, don't alternate.** A newsletter that does finance one week and history the next has two half-audiences. One that does finance-through-history every week has one whole audience.

## Cross-cutting laws

Patterns that hold across every operator above.

**1. Narrow beats broad.** Every single one of these has a lens a reader could describe in one sentence. None of them "write about business."

**2. Cadence is the product.** Daily, weekly, whatever — kept. The ones with the largest audiences are almost universally the ones with the longest unbroken streaks. Frequency matters less than reliability.

**3. Voice is the moat, information is the commodity.** Information can be replicated in minutes. A specific person's judgment, humor, and taste cannot.

**4. One idea per issue.** Even the 8,000-word pieces are one argument, developed. Multi-item formats (Levine, Morning Brew) are the exception and only work at high frequency.

**5. Take a position.** Every one of these writers is willing to be wrong in public. Balanced non-commitment produces nothing worth forwarding.

**6. The archive compounds.** Link back constantly. Name your recurring ideas. The body of work becomes worth more than any post in it.

**7. Formats are containers, not cages.** Fixed structure removes decisions so the energy goes into thinking. Every one of these publications is instantly recognizable by shape alone.

**8. Original inputs.** Interviews, data, personal experience, or genuine domain expertise. Synthesis of public commentary has a very low ceiling.

**9. Monetize the relationship, not just the words.** Paid tiers, community, courses, funds, jobs, sponsorships, consulting. The list is the asset; the newsletter is how you earn it.
