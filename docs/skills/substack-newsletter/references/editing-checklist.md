# Editing

Four passes, in order. Don't interleave them — structural editing while line editing is how writers spend an hour polishing a section they should have deleted.

---

## Pass 1 — Structure

Read the whole draft without touching a sentence. Then answer:

1. **What is the one idea?** Say it in a sentence. If you can't, the piece isn't finished thinking yet. If you name three ideas, cut two and go deeper on the survivor — a fully developed idea beats three gestures at ideas every time.
2. **Where does it actually start?** Usually paragraph 3–5. Delete everything before it.
3. **Where does it sag?** Mark the section you'd skim if it weren't yours. Cut or compress it.
4. **Is the best material buried?** Writers routinely bury the most interesting line in the middle. Find it and consider promoting it to the hook or a subhead.
5. **Does the ending land or stop?** A piece that stops when the writer ran out is obvious to readers.
6. **Is there a real turn after the hook?** If the reader has no reason to finish by paragraph four, add one.

---

## Pass 2 — Line

Now go sentence by sentence.

**Cut:**
- Adverbs doing a verb's job: "walked quickly" → "hurried"
- Hedges that weaken claims you actually believe: "I think maybe," "somewhat," "it could be argued," "arguably," "in some sense"
- Filler openers: "In order to," "It's important to note that," "The fact that," "What's interesting is that," "At the end of the day," "Needless to say"
- Any sentence that only announces the next sentence

**Convert:**
- Passive → active where the actor matters
- Abstraction → specific. Every abstract claim needs a concrete within two sentences.
- Nominalizations → verbs: "made a decision" → "decided," "provides assistance" → "helps"
- Long paragraphs → 1–3 sentences each
- Label subheads → statement subheads: "Distribution" → "Distribution is the moat"

**Check rhythm.** Read aloud. Every stumble is a reader's stumble. Land key claims in short sentences — a long sentence followed by a four-word one is the most reliable emphasis device in prose.

---

## Pass 3 — Remove the machine tells

Whether or not AI was involved, these patterns make writing sound generic. They are the fastest way to lose a reader's sense that a person is present.

**Delete or rewrite:**

- **The rule-of-three tic.** Every list having exactly three items, every sentence with three parallel clauses. Vary it — use two, use five.
- **"It's not just X, it's Y."** And its cousins: "This isn't about X. It's about Y." Once per piece, maximum. Usually zero.
- **Symmetrical antithesis everywhere.** "Not a bug, but a feature." "Less a strategy than a symptom."
- **Em-dash overuse.** They're fine; a paragraph with three of them isn't.
- **Empty intensifiers:** "truly," "genuinely," "deeply," "profoundly," "fundamentally," "incredibly"
- **Corporate-mystical vocabulary:** "leverage," "unlock," "navigate the landscape," "at the intersection of," "in an era of," "the reality is"
- **Summary paragraphs that restate what you just said.** Trust the reader.
- **Balanced non-conclusions.** "There are compelling arguments on both sides." Take a side or don't raise the question.
- **Bulleted lists where prose belongs.** Bullets fragment argument. Use them for genuinely parallel items only.
- **Uniform paragraph length.** Real writing has ragged rhythm.

**Add back the person:**

- A specific detail only you would know
- An opinion you'd defend at dinner
- Something you're uncertain about, said plainly
- The word "I," at least a few times
- A joke, if that's your register — and if it isn't, don't force one

---

## Pass 4 — Pre-send

- [ ] Subject line: 8–10 candidates written, best one chosen, under ~50 characters
- [ ] Preview text set explicitly (not defaulting to the first body line)
- [ ] Title and subtitle set — subtitle states a claim, not a topic
- [ ] Every link tested
- [ ] Images have alt text; charts are readable at phone width
- [ ] Names, numbers, dates, and quotes verified against source
- [ ] Exactly one CTA
- [ ] Test send to yourself; read it on a phone
- [ ] Formatting survives dark mode
- [ ] Nothing you'd regret if the subject were reading it
- [ ] 3–5 Notes or social posts drafted from the issue

---

## Reviewing someone else's draft

Order matters. Give feedback in this sequence:

1. **What's working, specifically.** Name the strongest paragraph and say why. This is more actionable than any critique, because it tells them what to do more of.
2. **One structural note.** The single biggest lever. Not five.
3. **Line notes.** Now, and only now.
4. **The one thing to change if they change nothing else.**

**Preserve their voice.** The goal is to make them sound more like themselves, not more like a well-edited newsletter. If a habit is unusual but works, leave it — the quirks are what readers subscribe to. Flag patterns rather than rewriting sentences, so they learn the edit instead of just receiving it.
