# Hooks, Subject Lines, Titles, Preview Text

Four different jobs, often confused. The subject line earns the open. The preview text extends it. The title earns the click on the web and does SEO work. The hook earns the second paragraph.

---

## Subject lines

**Specs.** 3–7 words. Roughly 30–50 characters — beyond that, mobile clients truncate. Sentence case reads more personal than Title Case for a solo newsletter; Title Case is fine for a brand publication. No emoji unless it's an established part of your voice. Never all caps.

**Write 8–10 candidates, then pick.** The first one you write is almost always the most generic. This is the single cheapest quality improvement available.

**Patterns that work:**

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete number | [Number] + [specific noun] | `The 11 to 5,800 playbook` |
| Contrarian claim | [Common belief] is wrong | `Open rates are lying to you` |
| Curiosity gap | Withhold the resolution | `What Stripe won't say out loud` |
| Named thing | Proper noun + angle | `Netflix's price problem` |
| Direct question | Reader's private question | `Should you go paid yet?` |
| Confession | Admission of failure | `I deleted 4,000 subscribers` |
| Plain descriptive | For high-trust lists | `Deep dive: Anthropic` |

**Things that kill open rates:**

- Vague abstraction: "Thoughts on growth," "Some updates"
- Clickbait the body doesn't pay off — trains readers to distrust you, and the damage compounds
- Your newsletter's name in every subject (wastes the scarcest characters you own; the sender name already does that job)
- "Issue #47" as the whole subject
- Manufactured urgency on a weekly send

**The trust rule.** Every subject line makes a promise. If the body doesn't keep it, you've bought one open at the cost of several future ones. Curiosity gaps are fine; lies are not.

**A note on personalization.** Merge-tag first names in subject lines have decayed badly — readers now read `{First name},` as a marketing signal rather than a personal one. Behavioral or contextual reference outperforms name-insertion by a wide margin.

## Preview text

The most wasted real estate in email. Default behavior is to pull the first line of the body, which is usually "Hi everyone" or a logo alt-text.

**Job:** a second headline that *extends* the subject line rather than repeating it.

- Subject: `Open rates are lying to you`
- Preview: `The three metrics that actually predict whether a newsletter survives`

**Specs:** 40–90 characters. Set it explicitly on every send. Never let it default.

---

## Titles (web / SEO)

On Substack the title also lives on the web, gets indexed, and appears in Notes and recommendation surfaces. It often should differ from the subject line.

- **Subject line optimizes for the inbox:** short, curious, personal.
- **Title optimizes for search and browse:** clearer, more descriptive, keyword-bearing.

Same post: subject `Should you go paid yet?` / title `When to launch a paid newsletter tier (and when not to)`.

**Subtitle / dek.** One sentence stating the promise explicitly. This is what appears under the title on the archive page and in most share cards — it does more work than writers assume. Make it a claim, not a description: "Paid conversion is a function of free-list trust, not content quality" beats "A look at paid conversion."

## Hooks (the first 1–3 sentences)

The reader has opened. You have about three seconds.

**The rule that fixes most drafts: cut the first two paragraphs.** Writers warm up. The real hook is usually already in the draft, sitting in paragraph three.

### Patterns

**1. The scene.** Drop the reader into a specific moment.
> In March 2023, a product manager at a company you've heard of sent a Slack message that cost her employer $40 million.

**2. The number.** Lead with something concrete and surprising.
> 61% of the subscribers who joined my list last year have never opened an email.

**3. The flat contradiction.** State a claim against consensus, without hedging.
> Consistency is overrated. Almost every newsletter I've studied that broke 100,000 subscribers did it in bursts.

**4. The private question.** Something the reader has wondered but not said.
> You've probably asked yourself whether anyone would notice if you stopped publishing.

**5. The confession.** Admit something costly.
> I spent four months writing to 200 people and told everyone it was going great.

**6. The strange fact.** Present something odd in flat language and let it sit.
> There is a company in Ohio whose entire business is buying other people's expired warranties.

**7. The direct address.** Name the reader's situation precisely.
> If you're deciding this quarter whether to hire a VP of Sales, this is the piece I wish someone had sent me.

**8. In medias res.** Start mid-action, explain later.
> The email went out at 6:04am. By 6:40 the unsubscribe link had been clicked 1,100 times.

### Openings to delete on sight

- "In today's fast-paced world..." and every variant
- "I've been thinking a lot about..."
- "Hi everyone! Hope you're all doing well."
- "Sorry it's been a while since the last issue" — nobody noticed; you just told them
- Defining a term before establishing why anyone should care
- Explaining what the newsletter is going to cover *before* giving a reason to want it
- Any sentence whose only content is that you're about to say something

### The hook test

Read the first three sentences alone. Would a stranger who doesn't know you keep reading? If the only reason to continue is that they subscribed, the hook isn't doing its job.

## The turn

The paragraph after the hook, and the most commonly missing piece in amateur drafts. The hook creates attention; the turn converts it into a *reason to finish*.

The turn names the tension: why the obvious answer is wrong, what's at stake, or what you'll prove. Two to four sentences.

> [Hook: the number]
> That should have been a catastrophe. It wasn't — the list grew 12% that month, and the reason has nothing to do with email and everything to do with how Substack's recommendation engine now weights engagement over volume.

---

## Titles for repeatable series

If you're building a signature series, the title format should be identical and instantly recognizable: `Deep Dive: [Company]`, `How [Person] grew to [Number]`, `[Number] things I learned about [Topic]`.

Consistent series titles make an archive legible, which makes new readers binge, which is how a single good post turns into a subscription.
